Karnataka Stamp Act by Act No.8 of 1995 came into effect

It would be relevant to note that in the present case, 
time is not the essence of contract. In the plaint, plaintiff 
has clearly stated that he was always ready and willing to 
perform his part of the contract. The law is well settled 
that if plaintiff clearly states that he is ready and willing 
perform his part of the contract and that defendant evades 
the execution of the registered sale deed, then plaintiff is 
entitled to the relief of specific performance.  
 
In the present case, admittedly, defendant No.1 has 
sold the property during the pendency of the suit. 
Therefore, once the property is sold during the pendency 
of the suit, what has to be looked into is whether the 
purchaser is the bonafide purchaser for value without 
notice.  
 
Both the Courts below concurrently held that 
defendant No.3 is not a bonafide purchaser for  valur 
without  notice and knowledge of Ex.P1 - agreement for

sale.  I find no reason to interfere with the concurrent

In the last resort, counsel for defendant No.3 
contended that the First Appellate Court ought to have 
allowed the application (I.A.No.2) for additional evidence 
and remanded the matter to the trial Court. 
 
I have considered the said contention with care. It is 
true that defendant No.3 had filed I.A.No.2 under Order 
41 Rule 27 of CPC seeking permission to produce certain 
documents. The First Appellate Court rejected the 
application on the ground that the material available on 
record was sufficient to answer the issues and decide the 
case effectively. Therefore, the additional evidence is not 
required to decide the dispute in controversy between the 
parties.  
 
It would be relevant to note that under Rule 27 of 
Order 41 of CPC, production of additional evidence, 
whether oral or documentary is permitted only under three 
circumstances which are: 1) the trial Court had refused to 


--- PAGE 15 ---
15 
 
admit the evidence though it ought to have been admitted 
2) the evidence was not available to the party, despite 
exercise of due diligence; and 3) the Appellate Court 
required additional evidence so as to enable it to 
pronounce better judgment or for any other substantial 
cause of like nature. The true test is whether the appellate 
Court is able to pronounce judgment on the materials 
before it without taking into consideration the additional 
evidence sought to be adduced. When the appellate Court 
finds itself unable to pronounce judgment owing to a 
lacuna or defect in the evidence as it stands, it may admit 
additional evidence. But, in the present case, learned 
Judge found that the additional evidence is not required to 
decide the dispute.  
 
I propose to say that the production and leading 
evidence in appellate Court, oral or documentary, is not a 
matter of right, or ipso facto. I would observe that the 
parties can produce evidence only when the conditions laid 
down in Rule 27 are satisfied. It is perhaps well to observe 
that First Appellate Court’s discretion in admitting or not 
admitting additional evidence will not ordinarily be

16 
 
interfered with in second appeal. I find it necessary only to 
say this much that I am not prepared to differ from the 
view taken by the trial Court and by the First Appellate 
Court as to the question of fact. 
 
Reverting back to the facts of the case, learned trial 
Judge in extenso referred to the evidence on record and 
held that plaintiff has proved the agreement for sale and 
directed that defendants 1 to 3 to execute the sale deed in 
favor of plaintiff. On appeal, the Appellate Judge                    
re-appreciated the evidence and has confirmed the 
judgment and decree of the trial Court. In my view, the 
First Appellate Court has re-appreciated the material on 
record in right perspective.  
 
 
It is perhaps well to observe here that the after the 
1976 amendment, the scope of Section 100 of CPC has 
been drastically curtailed and narrowed down. The High 
Court would have jurisdiction of interfering under Section 
100 of CPC only in a case where substantial question of 
law is involved and those questions have been clearly 
formulated in the memorandum of appeal.
